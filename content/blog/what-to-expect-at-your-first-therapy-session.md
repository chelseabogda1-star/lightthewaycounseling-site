One of the most common reasons people put off making the call is that they have no idea what they're walking into. Movies are not much help. So here is the unglamorous version of what a first session actually looks like.

## Before you arrive

You'll usually complete intake paperwork ahead of time: contact information, insurance, a health history, consent forms, and privacy notices. It is tedious, and it exists so your clinician doesn't spend your first appointment doing data entry.

Somewhere in that packet you'll find a form about confidentiality. It's worth reading. Therapy is confidential, with a small set of legally required exceptions — mainly concerns about immediate danger to you or someone else, and suspected abuse of a child or a vulnerable adult. Your clinician will go over this with you directly at the start of the session.

## The first ten minutes

Most clinicians open by explaining how they work, what confidentiality means in practice, and how long sessions run. Then they'll ask some version of: *what brought you in?*

You do not need a polished answer. "I don't really know, things have just felt heavy" is a completely acceptable place to start. Part of the clinician's job is to help you put language to something you have not had words for yet.

## What you'll be asked

A first session is mostly information gathering. Expect questions about:

- What's been going on recently, and how long it's been going on
- Sleep, appetite, energy, concentration
- Who's in your life — family, partners, friends, work or school
- Any previous therapy or medication, and how that went
- What you're hoping is different six months from now

Some clinicians use a structured assessment; others keep it conversational. Both are normal.

## What you don't have to do

You do not have to tell your whole story in the first hour. You are allowed to say "I'm not ready to get into that yet." A good clinician will hear that as useful information rather than resistance.

You also don't have to arrive with a goal. Plenty of people start therapy because something feels off and they can't name it. Naming it can be the first few months of work.

## How it ends

Toward the end, your clinician will usually offer some initial thoughts — what they're noticing, how they'd approach the work, and what a reasonable next step looks like. You'll talk about frequency. Weekly is the most common starting point, because momentum matters early on; some people move to every other week later.

## How to tell whether it's a fit

Decades of research on therapy outcomes keep landing on the same finding: the relationship between client and therapist predicts results better than the specific method used. That makes fit a practical question, not a picky one.

After a session or two, it's reasonable to ask yourself:

- Did I feel listened to, or managed?
- Did anything they said land in a way that felt accurate?
- Could I imagine telling this person something embarrassing?
- Did I leave feeling slightly lighter, or at least clearer?

A first session can feel awkward and still be a good fit — awkwardness is mostly a function of talking to a stranger about hard things. But if after three or four sessions you're consistently feeling unseen, say so. Clinicians would genuinely rather help you find a better match than have you quietly stop coming.

## If you're bringing a child or teen

For younger clients, the first appointment often includes parents for part or all of the session, then shifts to time with the child alone. Teens in particular need to know what will and won't be shared with parents; your clinician should set that out clearly with everyone in the room, so your teen isn't guessing.

## A reasonable first step

If you're in the Fox Valley and considering starting, you don't have to commit to a course of therapy to make an appointment. You're allowed to come once, see how it feels, and decide from there.
