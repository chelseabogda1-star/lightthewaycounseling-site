It usually starts small. A stomachache on a Tuesday. Then two mornings a week. Then a child standing in the hallway, genuinely unable to walk out the door, while you are already late for work and out of ideas.

School refusal is one of the most exhausting things a family can go through, partly because every instinct you have — reason with them, insist, take away the phone — tends to make it worse.

## It's usually anxiety, not defiance

The distinction matters because it changes what works. Truancy is typically about wanting to be somewhere else. School refusal is about needing to *not* be at school — and the child usually wants to want to go.

Underneath it, there's almost always something specific:

- Social anxiety, or a specific fear about the cafeteria, the bus, PE, or being called on
- Separation anxiety, often worse after an illness, a loss, or family stress
- An undiagnosed learning difference, so school feels like daily public failure
- Bullying, or one relationship that's gone bad
- Sensory overwhelm, common for autistic kids in loud, unpredictable buildings
- Depression, where getting out of bed at all is the obstacle

Finding out which one is the first real task. Children often can't answer "why don't you want to go?" — not because they're hiding it, but because they don't know.

## Why avoidance grows

Anxiety operates on a simple loop: avoiding the feared thing produces immediate relief, and relief is a powerful reinforcer. Every morning a child stays home, the anxiety gets a little more convincing and school gets a little more frightening. This is why the problem tends to escalate rather than resolve on its own, and why waiting it out rarely works.

It's also why the goal is rarely "get them to school today at all costs." It's "rebuild tolerance in steps small enough to succeed."

## What tends to help

**Get eyes on the medical side first.** Rule out what's ruleable — headaches, stomach problems, sleep. It's rarely the whole story, but it should be off the table.

**Make home boring.** Not punitive — boring. If staying home means screens and comfort, the loop strengthens. Home on a school day should look as close to a school day as the household can manage.

**Return in graded steps.** For a child who's been out for weeks, walking back into a full day is usually too big a jump. A staircase looks something like: drive to the building, then sit in the office for twenty minutes, then one favorite class, then a half day. Each step stays in place until it's boring, then you move up.

**Get the school on the same page.** A named safe adult, a check-in point, a discreet exit plan for panic, and a 504 plan or IEP if a learning difference is in play. Most schools have seen this before and have more flexibility than parents expect.

**Address parent anxiety too.** Watching your child in distress and sending them in anyway is genuinely hard. Parents often need support for that, and it isn't a side issue — a parent who can stay calm and steady in the doorway is one of the strongest predictors of the plan working.

**Aim for progress, not streaks.** There will be bad mornings after good weeks. That's the shape of the work, not evidence it isn't working.

## When to bring in a clinician

Sooner than most families do. Rough markers: missed days are stacking up over two or more weeks, the morning routine has become a daily crisis, your child's world is shrinking beyond school, or you find yourself negotiating for hours.

Treatment typically pairs exposure-based work for the child with coaching for parents, plus coordination with the school. It is often shorter than parents fear — the point is to interrupt the avoidance loop, not to overhaul a personality.
